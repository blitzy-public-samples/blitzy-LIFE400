# Target Language Decision Matrix

This document names the language and runtime the LIFE400 core is to be rewritten in, and the datastore that follows from that choice. It is the document that answers the second stated business driver, because that driver — acquire engineering talent more familiar with a modern language — cannot be satisfied while "modern language" remains the unresolved variable. A sibling document establishes the gap this argues from: [the skills inventory and gap analysis](01-skills-inventory-and-gap.md) records what the estate demands today and why the pool able to supply it is narrower than the COBOL pool generally. This document establishes what to demand instead. The separation matters, because the gap is measured against the estate while the answer is measured against the labour market, and the two rest on entirely different evidence.

**Scope.** It owns three things and nothing else: the hard filter a candidate must pass before its other merits are weighed, the weighted criteria and the matrix that ranks five candidates against them, and the resulting primary recommendation with its runner-up and the reasons the remaining candidates are set aside. It does **not** decide where the resulting system runs — hosting and the exit from the current hardware are deliberately deferred in [MOD-ADR-007](../decisions/MOD-ADR-007-hardware-exit-deferred.md), and [the platform and support status document](../current-state/03-platform-and-support-status.md) sets out why conflating the two is a documented failure mode. It does **not** choose a user-interface component library or design system; none is specified anywhere in this assessment, and that selection is deferred as an open gap by [the UI modernization document](../target-state/05-ui-modernization.md). It does **not** perform the column-by-column type mapping, which belongs to [the target data model and schema mapping](../target-state/03-target-data-model-and-schema-mapping.md). And it names no supplier: conversion-tooling maturity is weighed here as a property of an ecosystem, whereas procuring a tool is a commercial decision the requirement does not authorise.

**The success criteria this document is measured against are owned elsewhere.** They are `SC-2.1` through `SC-2.9` in [the business drivers and success criteria](../01-business-drivers-and-success-criteria.md), which also fixes what "a modern mainstream language" is taken to mean for this assessment. Three of them are discharged here in particular: `SC-2.1`, that exactly one language and runtime is named and no other document names a different one; `SC-2.2`, that the claim of a deep enterprise labour pool rests on attributed third-party evidence rather than on assertion; and `SC-2.4`, that monetary arithmetic in the target is exact decimal at the precision the legacy system uses. The ambiguity register entry that pre-registers this outcome, `A-01`, is owned there as well. This document is the analysis behind that resolution and does not restate the register.

**Reading the citations.** A citation of the form `[<path>:<locator>]` is plain text rather than a hyperlink and points at a path in this repository. It stays meaningful in a diff and does not depend on a hosting provider's line-anchor syntax. Every range cited below was opened and read, and no source member was altered to produce this document. Platform terms link to [the IBM i glossary](../reference/glossary-ibm-i.md) on first use here.

**Attribution of external figures.** This document depends on information that is not in this repository more heavily than any other in the set, because a labour market cannot be read out of source code. Every such figure below names its publisher in the same sentence that uses it. None is presented as a measurement of this organisation, none is an estimate or projection for this programme, and where a publisher states a range it is reproduced as a range. Several of the sources below are vendors with a commercial interest in the figures they publish; that is disclosed at the point of use rather than left for the reader to infer. No cost, effort, duration or staffing figure appears anywhere in this document.

**This document is analysis, not a decision record.** It produces the reasoning two records rest on — [MOD-ADR-001 on the target language and runtime](../decisions/MOD-ADR-001-target-language-and-runtime.md) and [MOD-ADR-003 on the target datastore](../decisions/MOD-ADR-003-target-datastore.md) — and it therefore carries no status of its own. If the recommendation is overturned, the record is superseded and this analysis becomes the evidence the superseding record argues against, which is why the rationale lives in one place rather than being restated in every document that depends on it.

**No temporal content appears below.** No date, duration, ordering or calendar of work is stated or implied. Release cadence is described as a long-term-support line rather than by version number, so the document does not go stale. And no claim here rests on a build: nothing in this environment can compile, bind or run LIFE400, for the reasons [the platform and support status document](../current-state/03-platform-and-support-status.md) establishes.

## The hard filter: exact decimal arithmetic

A filter that eliminates candidates outright must be applied before anything is weighed. Presenting exact decimal arithmetic as one weighted row among many would be a logical error, because it would let a candidate accumulate enough points elsewhere to score its way past a correctness requirement. So it is applied first, on its own, and only the candidates it admits are eligible for the core. The weights that follow decide between those; they are shown for all five candidates so the margin is visible and the rejections are auditable rather than asserted.

### Why the precision is a business invariant rather than a type-system artifact

The strongest fact available is not that LIFE400 handles money. It is that the same precision is declared **twice, independently, in two different languages**, and the two declarations agree.

On the stored side, [DDS](../reference/glossary-ibm-i.md#dds-data-description-specifications) declares the monetary columns of the policy master as [zoned decimal](../reference/glossary-ibm-i.md#zoned-decimal) at fifteen digits with two decimals: the sum assured [QDDSSRC/POLMST.pf:L52], the policy loan balance [QDDSSRC/POLMST.pf:L54], the total annual premium [QDDSSRC/POLMST.pf:L59], the modal premium [QDDSSRC/POLMST.pf:L61] and the outstanding premium [QDDSSRC/POLMST.pf:L63]. The `S` in `15S 2` is the DDS type letter for zoned decimal rather than a per-column sign annotation; what the platform's sign handling then does with those columns is a question [the current data model](../current-state/04-data-model-current-state.md) owns and settles, and nothing in this document turns on it.

On the program side, the shared [copybook](../reference/glossary-ibm-i.md#copybook) declares the same quantities as thirteen integer digits plus two decimals — the same fifteen-digit, two-decimal precision written the other way. The premium-results group carries seven such items [QCPYSRC/POLDATA.cpy:L98-L105], the sum assured is declared the same way [QCPYSRC/POLDATA.cpy:L76], and so are the per-plan sum-assured bounds the underwriting rules test against [QCPYSRC/POLDATA.cpy:L42-L43].

Two independent declarations of one precision, in two languages, maintained by different mechanisms, is the signature of a business invariant rather than of one file's type system. That is why the requirement survives the migration: whatever the target stores money in has to reproduce fifteen digits and two decimals exactly, because both sides of the legacy system already agree that is what money is here. The field-level enumeration of every column and every contract item, and the analysis of where the two sides diverge, belong to [the current data model](../current-state/04-data-model-current-state.md) and are not re-derived here.

### The concrete stake: the one signed field

Exactly one item in the whole shared contract carries a leading `S` in its picture clause, and it is `PM-PREMIUM-DELTA` [QCPYSRC/POLDATA.cpy:L106]. Among the contract's numeric items it is the sole one declared able to hold a negative value, and what it holds is the amendment repricing delta — the difference between a policy's new total annual premium and its old one.

That is not an abstract correctness concern, because the estate does three things with it:

- It computes the delta on the batch servicing path, in the repricing [paragraph](../reference/glossary-ibm-i.md#paragraph) `3100-REPRICE-POLICY`, as the new total annual premium minus the old [QCBLLESRC/SVCBILB.cbl:L428-L429].
- It computes the delta a second time on the online path, from the same two quantities [QCBLLESRC/SVCMNT.cbl:L421-L422]. The two paths duplicating each other rather than sharing one implementation is a structural liability [the current-state architecture](../current-state/02-architecture-current-state.md) owns; what matters here is only that money arithmetic runs in both.
- It then puts the online delta on a screen, moving it into a display field in the result-display paragraph [QCBLLESRC/SVCMNT.cbl:L442], and stores it in the servicing file [QDDSSRC/SVCPF.pf:L45].

So the single place this system is declared able to represent a negative amount [QCPYSRC/POLDATA.cpy:L106] is the figure an operator reads back to a policyholder whose premium has just changed [QCBLLESRC/SVCMNT.cbl:L442]. A representation error there does not surface as a rounding artifact in a log. It surfaces as a disputed charge. One sentence is enough to make the requirement concrete, and this is it.

### What the arithmetic actually does

A storage type alone would not settle the question, because the numbers are computed rather than merely held. Across the eight [ILE](../reference/glossary-ibm-i.md#ile-integrated-language-environment) COBOL programs there are seventy-one `COMPUTE` statements, and the ones that produce premiums have a shape that is precisely where binary floating point diverges from exact decimal.

- The base annual premium divides the sum assured by one thousand and then multiplies the result by five separate four-decimal rating factors in a single expression [QCBLLESRC/NBUWB.cbl:L389-L395], with a second division by one thousand in the flat-extra adjustment that follows [QCBLLESRC/NBUWB.cbl:L397-L399].
- The tax amount multiplies a fifteen-digit, two-decimal money value by a four-decimal rate [QCBLLESRC/NBUWB.cbl:L443-L444], where the rate itself is declared with four decimal places [QCPYSRC/POLDATA.cpy:L52], and the product is received by a field with two.
- The modal premium divides the total annual premium by a billing divisor — one, two, four or twelve — and then multiplies by a four-decimal loading factor [QCBLLESRC/NBUWB.cbl:L462-L464]. The online program repeats the identical expression [QCBLLESRC/NBUWMNT.cbl:L469-L471], and the batch servicing program computes the tax the same way again [QCBLLESRC/SVCBILB.cbl:L518-L519].

Division by twelve does not terminate in decimal, so every one of those expressions produces an intermediate value with more decimal places than the field receiving it. What happens next is the part that raises the bar on the target, and it is established by exhaustive search rather than inference: **no `COMPUTE` in the estate carries a `ROUNDED` phrase, and no arithmetic statement carries an `ON SIZE ERROR` clause.** A search of all eight COBOL programs, all five ILE [CL](../reference/glossary-ibm-i.md#cl-control-language) programs, the copybook and all ten DDS members returns no occurrence of either. Every result is therefore truncated to the receiving field's declared scale by language default, silently.

The consequence for language selection is sharper than "money needs decimals". The target needs an exact decimal facility in which **scale and rounding behaviour are explicit and controllable at each operation**, because reproducing this system's outputs means reproducing truncation at a declared scale, not applying whatever rounding a default supplies. Scale is not uniform either — a four-decimal flat extra rate is stored alongside two-decimal money [QDDSSRC/POLMST.pf:L49], and the rating factors are four-decimal in the contract [QCPYSRC/POLDATA.cpy:L85-L87] — so a single fixed money type is insufficient and a configurable-scale decimal type is required. The count of distinct scales and their file-side counterparts are owned by [the current data model](../current-state/04-data-model-current-state.md).

Why this matters at all rather than being a curiosity: output parity against the legacy system is the acceptance condition for the migration, and the parity comparison is field-level. The strategy that makes that comparison possible is owned by [the characterization test strategy](../migration/05-characterization-test-strategy.md), and the reason it is indispensable here is that the estate contains no test member of any kind against which a conversion could otherwise be checked.

### The rule that follows

The target must offer an exact decimal type for money, with explicit scale and explicit rounding, and must never carry a monetary value through binary floating point. The reason is one line of arithmetic:

```text
exact decimal at scale 2   0.10 + 0.20  ->  0.30                 reproduces the stored value
binary floating point      0.10 + 0.20  ->  0.30000000000000004  differs in the last place
truncated into 15S 2       a last-place difference is a parity failure, not a display artifact
```

The representation decision itself is recorded in [MOD-ADR-006 on date and decimal representation](../decisions/MOD-ADR-006-date-and-decimal-representation.md), which also carries the companion consequence that the estate's eight-digit `YYYYMMDD` date integers — the paid-to date [QDDSSRC/POLMST.pf:L71] among them, and the whole date group of the shared contract [QCPYSRC/POLDATA.cpy:L108-L115] — become real date types in the target. The date argument is not developed here; it belongs to that record and to [the target schema mapping](../target-state/03-target-data-model-and-schema-mapping.md).

### Applying the filter, candidate by candidate

Every one of the five candidates has *some* exact-decimal facility, so stating the filter as an availability test would be both factually weak and easy to refute. What it actually discriminates is narrower and more decisive: whether the exact decimal type is a first-class part of the language or its standard library, whether it is the *idiomatic default* for business arithmetic rather than an opt-out from a binary floating-point default held by convention at every site, and whether the mainstream database-access layer maps a fixed-scale decimal column to it without being told to.

| Candidate | Exact decimal comes from | Is it the idiomatic default for money? | Fixed-scale column maps to it by default? | Filter verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Java | The standard library, as an arbitrary-precision decimal type that carries its scale as part of the value and refuses an inexact division outright unless given a rounding mode | Yes — it is the conventional money type, and a division that would round silently fails instead, which forces the truncation decision to be written down | Yes — the standard database interface and the mainstream persistence layers map a decimal column to it without configuration | **Passes without conditions** |
| C#/.NET | The language itself, as a built-in decimal type with its own numeric literal form, tracking scale through arithmetic | Yes — it is the conventional money type, and the literal form makes an accidental binary value a visible mistake | Yes — the mainstream data-access and object-relational layers map a decimal column to it without configuration | **Passes without conditions** |
| Python | The standard library, as a decimal type with an explicit context carrying both precision and rounding mode — the most explicit rounding control of the five | No — the language's own numeric literal and default arithmetic are binary floating point, so exactness depends on a convention held at every arithmetic site and every module boundary | Largely — the mainstream driver and object-relational layers return the decimal type for a fixed-scale column by default | **Passes only under a standing discipline** |
| TypeScript/Node | A third-party library; neither the language nor its standard library provides one, and the built-in arbitrary-precision integer type cannot carry a scale | No — the sole built-in numeric type is binary floating point, and it is what every arithmetic operator and every untyped library boundary produces | No — drivers commonly surface a fixed-scale column as a string or as a binary number, and preserving exactness requires deliberate configuration at each one | **Does not pass** |
| Go | A third-party library; the language has no decimal type and the standard database interface has none to scan into | No — the built-in numeric types are binary integers and binary floats | No — scanning a fixed-scale column into a decimal requires the third-party library's own scanner, wired up per column | **Does not pass** |

The filter therefore narrows the field to two candidates that pass without conditions, admits a third only if a convention is maintained indefinitely by every engineer who touches the money paths, and excludes two. It does not say that a correct system cannot be built in the excluded languages — it can, with discipline and a dependency. It says that in those languages exactness is something a team must remember, whereas in the two that pass it is what happens when nobody is thinking about it. For a codebase whose acceptance test is field-level output parity, and which arrives with no test member anywhere in the estate to catch a lapse — an absence established by [the characterization test strategy](../migration/05-characterization-test-strategy.md) — that difference is the whole point.

Every criterion below serves Driver 1 (minimize security risk), Driver 2 (acquire engineering talent more familiar with a modern mainstream language), or both. A criterion serving neither would not belong in a matrix answering these two drivers, however interesting it might be on its own, and none is included.

These three carry the heaviest weights because each is load-bearing for this estate specifically rather than for modernization in general.

### Scoring scale and the status of these weights

- Each candidate is scored on each criterion from `1` to `5`, where `1` means the criterion is poorly served, `3` means adequately served with a material caveat, and `5` means served without reservation. Integers only; no half-points, because a half-point would imply a precision the underlying judgement does not have.
- Weights sum to `100`, so a weighted total falls between `100` and `500` and the arithmetic can be checked by hand from the table.
- **The weights are a documented judgement, not a measurement.** Nothing in this repository determines that exact-decimal ecosystem maturity deserves twenty points and batch maturity six. What the repository determines is that both are relevant and which is more consequential for this estate; the numbers express that ordering so it can be argued with. A reader who disagrees can substitute their own and recompute, which is the point of publishing the weights rather than only the conclusion.
- The scores are judgements too, but of a different kind: each is defended in the candidate evaluation below, and the ones resting on external evidence name their publisher there.

Four properties of that result are worth stating plainly, including the two that cut against the conclusion.

- **The top two positions are insensitive to any single criterion.** Removing any one of the ten criteria entirely and recomputing leaves the first and second places unchanged. The recommendation therefore does not depend on one contested weight, which is the property that makes it defensible rather than merely arithmetic.
- **The gap between third, fourth and fifth is not a meaningful separation.** Twenty-eight points separate third from fourth and four points separate fourth from fifth, on a five-hundred-point scale. Those three candidates are best read as one group the filter had already placed outside the set that passes without conditions — one conditionally, two not at all — rather than as a ranked tail. Reporting the order without that caveat would overstate what the arithmetic supports.
- **The leading candidate scores worst of the five on developer desirability.** That is the honest cost of the recommendation and it is scored, not hidden. The reason it does not change the outcome is that `C9` carries five points while `C2` and `C3` carry thirty-five between them, and Driver 2 asks for a pool that can be hired from rather than for the language engineers most want to use.
- **Popularity is scored nowhere.** No criterion rewards a language for total usage share. That is deliberate: the ecosystem with the largest overall usage share is among the candidates the filter excludes, and a matrix that rewarded usage share would have surfaced it as a contender for a policy-administration core it is unsuited to.

## Candidate evaluation

Each candidate is assessed on its own terms and ends in a verdict. A matrix that strawmans the options it rejects is not auditable, so each subsection states what the candidate is genuinely good at before saying why it does or does not win this particular decision.

### Java

Java passes the filter without conditions. Its standard-library decimal type is the conventional representation for money, it carries its scale as part of the value rather than as a formatting concern, and it will not perform an inexact division at all unless the caller names a rounding mode. That last property is unusual and directly useful here: the estate truncates silently at a declared scale in every premium calculation [QCBLLESRC/NBUWB.cbl:L443-L444], and a target that forces the truncation decision to be written down is a target in which that behaviour can be reproduced deliberately instead of inherited by accident.

On labour-pool depth the evidence is specific to the right vertical rather than to popularity. OpenLogic's *State of Open Source Report* finds that of all industries surveyed, the banking, insurance and financial-services sector reports the highest Java usage, closely followed by government and public services, and that Spring adoption is likewise highest in that same sector at 47.22 per cent. Azul's *State of Java Survey*, conducted by Dimensional Research across 2,039 qualified respondents on five continents, reports that 99 per cent of surveyed organisations actively use Java and that roughly seven in ten say more than half their applications run on Java or the Java Virtual Machine. Those are the figures that matter for `C2`, because they describe where the hireable pool for a transactional insurance core actually sits.

On conversion tooling the ecosystem is unambiguously the deepest of the five. SoftwareMining publishes a deterministic rule-based translator targeting Java; Astadia publishes an automated conversion and testing platform for the same target and states that other providers in the mainframe-modernization market automate 70 to 80 per cent of the process with the remaining 20 to 30 per cent done by hand — a vendor-published range about competitors, reproduced here as a range and not as a projection for this programme. Amazon Web Services describes its own transformation service as refactoring COBOL programs into Java Spring Boot applications, which is the closest published match to the recommended stack. Most relevant of all, Hakuna Matata Tech's survey of COBOL-to-Java conversion tools catalogues Fresche Solutions as specialising in transforming legacy IBM i COBOL and RPG applications into modern Java frameworks — the only entry in any catalogue consulted that names this platform family rather than the mainframe. No comparable catalogue of COBOL conversion tooling exists for any of the three remaining candidates.

Static typing and refactoring support are the strongest available, which matters more here than it would on a project with tests. Batch maturity is first-class: chunk-oriented, restartable batch processing with skip and retry semantics is a standard part of the ecosystem rather than a library to assemble, which is what replacing a nightly driver requires. Relational access maps a fixed-scale decimal column to the decimal type without configuration. Long-term-support lines are published with stated support windows, and multiple independent builds of the runtime exist, so patch supply does not depend on a single supplier — the direct counter to the exposure the current platform baseline creates [README.md:L264].

The honest cost is developer enthusiasm, and it is the one dimension Java loses on outright. The Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2025 — which drew more than 49,000 responses across 177 countries and 314 technologies — reports Java used by roughly 30 per cent of professional developers while only 17.9 per cent of respondents say they want to work with it, against an admiration rating of 47.6 per cent. That gap is real, it is why `C9` exists as a criterion, and it is why Java scores two rather than five on it. A second caution belongs here and is a Driver 1 caution rather than a Driver 2 one: OpenLogic's report also finds that 33 per cent of banking, insurance and financial-services organisations still run a web-server version that no longer receives community support or security patches. Choosing a language with a healthy support line does not by itself deliver patch supply — the upgrade discipline has to be maintained, and this recommendation should not be read as making that problem go away.

**Verdict.** Passes the filter without conditions and ranks first, on the three decisive criteria rather than on the supporting ones. Recommended for the core.

### C# and .NET

C#/.NET is a genuinely close second and should be read as a defensible alternative rather than as a foil. Its decimal type is built into the language with its own numeric literal form, which makes an accidentally binary monetary value a visible mistake in the source rather than a silent one — arguably a better ergonomic than the recommended candidate offers. Its mainstream data-access and object-relational layers map a fixed-scale decimal column to it without configuration, so it scores level with Java on `C8`. Its long-term-support cadence is published with stated windows, and its security, dependency-scanning and static-analysis tooling is equally mature, so it scores level on `C5` and `C6` as well. Static typing and automated refactoring are level on `C4`. On developer sentiment it does better than the recommended candidate, not worse: the TIOBE Index named C# its programming language of the year for 2025 and, on Keyhole Software's reading of that index, it is closing the gap with Java in business software.

Three things separate it in this matrix, and none of them is a deficiency in the language.

- On `C1`, Java's decimal type refuses an inexact division without an explicit rounding mode, whereas the .NET decimal applies a default. Both preserve scale and both are exact; the difference is only that one of them forces the rounding decision to be written down. Given that reproducing silent truncation at a declared scale is the actual requirement here [QCBLLESRC/NBUWB.cbl:L462-L464], being compelled to state the rounding at every division is a small but real advantage on this estate. On a system without that constraint the two would score the same.
- On `C2`, the published evidence that names the insurance vertical specifically points at Java. OpenLogic's industry breakdown is a Java report and has no C# counterpart consulted here, so the honest position is that C#'s enterprise pool is demonstrably deep while its *insurance back-office* depth is less directly evidenced — a gap in the available evidence as much as in the pool. That is why the score is four rather than five, and it is the criterion most likely to move if better evidence appears.
- On `C3`, the conversion tooling targeting C# is real but narrower. SoftwareMining publishes a COBOL-to-C# translator alongside its Java one, and Astadia names C# as a target for its AWS refactoring work, so the capability plainly exists. What has no C# counterpart in the catalogues consulted is an offering that names IBM i COBOL and RPG specifically.

**Verdict.** Passes the filter without conditions and ranks second. Recorded as the documented runner-up: if the primary recommendation is revisited, this is the candidate it should be revisited in favour of.

### TypeScript and Node

TypeScript is a serious language with excellent static typing, first-rate editor tooling and by far the largest pool of engineers who already know it — the Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2025 reports JavaScript as the most-used language at 66 per cent, and that pool is the reason it scores five on onboarding accessibility and four on desirability. None of that is in dispute.

It does not pass the filter, and the reason is structural rather than a matter of taste. The sole built-in numeric type is binary floating point, so it is what every arithmetic operator produces by default and what every untyped library boundary hands back. Exact decimal arithmetic is available only as a third-party dependency, and the language's arbitrary-precision integer type cannot carry a scale, so it does not substitute. That means correctness for the fifteen-digit, two-decimal monetary fields [QDDSSRC/POLMST.pf:L52] would rest on every developer, and every library on every code path, remembering not to use the default. The database boundary makes it worse rather than better: drivers commonly surface a fixed-scale column as a string or as a binary number, so exactness has to be re-established deliberately at each one, which is why it scores two on `C8`. Batch maturity is the other weak point at two: the runtime's single-threaded event loop is a poor fit for the long-running, restartable sweep the operational model requires. And there is no established COBOL-to-TypeScript conversion tooling in any catalogue consulted, which is why `C3` scores one.

The rejection must be scoped honestly, because it is narrower than it sounds. **It is a rejection for the core only.** Nothing here argues against TypeScript in a presentation tier, where no monetary arithmetic is performed and values arrive already computed and already rounded. That is a separate decision, it is not taken in this document, and it is deferred as an open gap by [the UI modernization document](../target-state/05-ui-modernization.md) — which also records that no component library or design system is specified anywhere in this assessment.

**Verdict.** Evaluated and rejected for the core. Not rejected for the presentation tier, where the decision is deferred and belongs to another document.

### Python

Python is the candidate it would be easiest to be unfair to, and the unfair version of the argument — that it cannot do exact decimals — is simply false. Its standard-library decimal type is excellent, and its explicit context carrying both precision and rounding mode is arguably the most direct rounding control of any candidate here, including the recommended one. Its mainstream driver and object-relational layers return that decimal type for a fixed-scale column by default, which is why it scores four on `C8` — better than two of the other candidates. It has a very large and growing labour pool; the Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2025 reports its usage rising by seven percentage points year on year, and it scores five on desirability accordingly. Its published releases carry stated security-fix windows.

The reasons it does not take the core are three, and they are precise.

- **The default numeric type is binary floating point.** A decimal literal in Python source is a binary float, so exactness is a convention rather than a property of the language — the same structural issue as TypeScript, differing in degree because the exact type is in the standard library rather than in a dependency and because the persistence layer defaults the right way. That is why the filter verdict is conditional rather than a failure, and why `C1` scores three rather than one or five.
- **Dynamic typing removes the safety net that matters most here.** This is the decisive supporting reason, and it is specific to this estate rather than a general preference. There is no test member anywhere in the estate, an absence established by [the characterization test strategy](../migration/05-characterization-test-strategy.md), so the conversion has no behavioural net beneath it. In a language where a rename or a signature change cannot be verified mechanically across the codebase, the only remaining check on a large-scale refactor is review. That is why `C4` scores two, and on a project that arrived with a comprehensive suite this criterion would carry far less against it.
- **COBOL conversion tooling depth is thin.** No catalogue consulted lists an established COBOL-to-Python translator comparable to the Java and C# offerings, so `C3` scores one.

Where it is genuinely strong is worth recording rather than omitting, because it is a real role in this programme: data reconciliation and migration-validation scripting during the parallel run — extract comparison, checksum and count reconciliation, exception triage — is work Python suits well, and nothing in this recommendation excludes it from that. Choosing a language for the transactional core is not the same as choosing every language used in the programme.

**Verdict.** Evaluated and rejected for the core. A legitimate supporting role in migration validation and reconciliation.

### Go

Go's strengths are real and pull in a direction this estate cares about. It is statically typed with fast, dependable tooling, and its concurrency and operational simplicity make it a strong choice for long-running services — which is why it scores four on batch and long-running-job maturity, ahead of two of the other candidates, level with a third, and behind only the recommended one. Its release policy is published, its security and dependency tooling is sound, and the Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2025 places it among the languages developers most want to work with, so it scores four on desirability.

It does not pass the filter for the most basic of reasons: the language has no decimal type, and neither does its standard library. Exact decimal arithmetic requires a third-party package, and the standard database interface has no decimal type to scan a fixed-scale column into, so each such column has to be wired to the package's own scanner explicitly. For a schema whose monetary columns are pervasive and whose scales are not uniform [QDDSSRC/POLMST.pf:L49], that is a per-column obligation rather than a one-time configuration, which is why `C8` scores two. On the two decisive labour criteria it is the weakest of the five: its pool is concentrated in infrastructure and platform engineering rather than insurance back-office systems, and no COBOL-to-Go conversion tooling appears in any catalogue consulted.

**Verdict.** Evaluated and not selected. Well suited to operational and infrastructure services; not suited to a policy-administration core whose acceptance condition is exact monetary parity.

## Primary recommendation and runner-up

**The primary recommendation is Java on a current long-term-support release, with Spring Boot and PostgreSQL.** The runner-up is C#/.NET. TypeScript/Node and Python are evaluated and rejected for the core. Go is evaluated and not selected.

The three decisive criteria that produce that outcome, named as such, are **exact decimal arithmetic**, **enterprise insurance labour-pool depth** and **COBOL-conversion tooling maturity**. Each is decisive for a different reason:

- Exact decimal arithmetic is decisive because it is a correctness gate rather than a preference. Money in this estate is fifteen digits with two decimals, declared independently on both sides of the same field [QDDSSRC/POLMST.pf:L52], [QCPYSRC/POLDATA.cpy:L98-L105], computed by truncating expressions with no rounding phrase anywhere in the source, and read back to policyholders through a single signed field [QCPYSRC/POLDATA.cpy:L106].
- Enterprise insurance labour-pool depth is decisive because it is the driver, restated as a measurable property. It is answered by attributed evidence — OpenLogic's finding that the banking, insurance and financial-services sector reports the highest Java usage of any industry it surveyed, and Azul's survey finding, conducted by Dimensional Research, that 99 per cent of surveyed organisations actively use Java — rather than by assertion, which is what `SC-2.2` requires of it.
- COBOL-conversion tooling maturity is decisive because the estate has no test member to guard a conversion, as [the characterization test strategy](../migration/05-characterization-test-strategy.md) establishes, so every hand-converted line is unguarded. The tooling ecosystem for this conversion targets Java and C# and effectively nothing else, and the only offering in any catalogue consulted that names IBM i COBOL specifically targets Java.

**The datastore half of the recommendation.** PostgreSQL is named for two reasons, one internal and one external. The internal reason is that the target needs a fixed-scale exact numeric column type that the language's decimal type maps to without loss, which is the `C8` criterion, and the transformation of each legacy column into such a type is owned by [the target schema mapping](../target-state/03-target-data-model-and-schema-mapping.md) rather than by this document. Persistence in the legacy system is defined entirely by DDS: three keyed physical files — the policy master [QDDSSRC/POLMST.pf:L81], the servicing file [QDDSSRC/SVCPF.pf:L54] and the claims file [QDDSSRC/CLMPF.pf:L67] — reached by record-level I/O, with no query language, no SQL definition and no schema artifact other than the DDS members themselves. The target datastore is therefore a new choice rather than a migration of an existing one. The external reason is labour-pool and ecosystem depth on the same footing as the language choice: the Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2025 reports PostgreSQL as the most-used database at 55.6 per cent of all respondents and 58.2 per cent of professional developers, and as leading both the most-admired and most-desired categories in successive editions of that survey, at roughly 65 and 46 per cent respectively. That half of the recommendation feeds [MOD-ADR-003](../decisions/MOD-ADR-003-target-datastore.md); the language and runtime half feeds [MOD-ADR-001](../decisions/MOD-ADR-001-target-language-and-runtime.md).

**Spring Boot** is named as the server-side application framework because it is where the batch and relational-access maturity scored under `C7` and `C8` actually lives, because OpenLogic's report places its adoption highest in the banking, insurance and financial-services sector at 47.22 per cent, and because Amazon Web Services describes its own COBOL transformation output as Spring Boot applications — which means the recommended stack is the one the conversion tooling already emits. It is a server-side runtime choice, not a user-interface choice, and it implies nothing about component selection.

**This recommendation is supersedable.** It is recorded as a decision record with a status, not asserted as a fact, and a later record may supersede it by naming a different language on better evidence; the criterion most likely to move is `C2`, where the insurance-vertical evidence for the runner-up is thinner than the pool probably is.

## Rejected candidates and the reasons

| Candidate | Filter verdict | Decisive reason it is not the core | Where it remains appropriate |
|---|---|---|---|
| TypeScript/Node | Does not pass | The only built-in numeric type is binary floating point and exact decimal is a third-party dependency, so correctness for the monetary fields would depend on convention at every arithmetic site and every driver boundary | A presentation tier performing no monetary arithmetic — a separate decision, deferred to [the UI modernization document](../target-state/05-ui-modernization.md) |
| Python | Passes only under a standing discipline | The exact-decimal facility is excellent but the default numeric type is binary floating point; decisively, dynamic typing removes the mechanical refactoring safety net that matters most in a conversion with no existing tests, and COBOL conversion tooling depth is thin | Data reconciliation, checksum and count comparison, and migration-validation scripting during the parallel run |
| Go | Does not pass | No decimal type in the language or its standard library, and no decimal type in the standard database interface to map a fixed-scale column to; weakest of the five on both decisive labour criteria | Operational and infrastructure services around the core, where its concurrency and deployment simplicity are genuine strengths |

None of these is rejected as a bad language, and none is rejected on a general preference. Each is rejected against criteria derived from this estate, and each rejection names the specific property that disqualifies it for a policy-administration core rather than gesturing at a category.

## What this decision does not settle

Naming a language settles less than it appears to, and four adjacent decisions are deliberately left open so that no reader mistakes one for the other.

- **Where the target runs.** Hosting is not decided here, and neither is the exit from the current hardware. Both are deferred in [MOD-ADR-007](../decisions/MOD-ADR-007-hardware-exit-deferred.md), and [the platform and support status document](../current-state/03-platform-and-support-status.md) explains why coupling a language decision to a hardware decision inflates scope and stalls work that could proceed on its own evidence. A language choice and a hardware choice rest on different evidence and have different blast radii.
- **What the user interface is built from.** No component library and no design system is named anywhere in this assessment. The estate's only interface is a fixed-format character interface on the [5250 datastream](../reference/glossary-ibm-i.md#5250-datastream), and mapping it to target screens — along with the component selection this document does not make — is owned by [the UI modernization document](../target-state/05-ui-modernization.md), which records the absence as an open gap rather than resolving it by implication.
- **How each column is typed.** This document establishes that the target requires a configurable-scale exact decimal type and a real date type. Which target type each of the legacy columns becomes, and what transformation each requires, is the column-by-column work owned by [the target schema mapping](../target-state/03-target-data-model-and-schema-mapping.md) and the representation decision recorded in [MOD-ADR-006](../decisions/MOD-ADR-006-date-and-decimal-representation.md).
- **Which migration strategy carries the conversion.** Choosing a target language is not choosing a route to it. The strategy archetypes, their evaluation against this estate and the selection among them are owned by [the strategy options and selection document](../migration/01-strategy-options-and-selection.md).

Two further boundaries are worth stating because crossing them would exceed what the requirement authorises. **No supplier is selected and no tool is procured** — conversion-tooling maturity is weighed here as a property of an ecosystem, and the vendors named above are named only as the publishers of figures this document attributes to them. **No regulatory framework is asserted to apply** to this system; whether any does is a question the business must confirm, and it is not answered here in either direction.
